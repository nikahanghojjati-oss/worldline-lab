# Field Note 001 · Distinguishing a time effect from a second history
Worldline Lab | Founding research session S-001 | 21 October 2026

Status: *Preliminary synthesis based on publisher abstracts, institutional summaries, and reference entry overviews*. This is not a line-by-line full-text review of every cited work. No research item is counted as fully studied on the basis of this document.

## The research question
Consider three logically distinct propositions:

**P1: Clock rates depend on conditions and worldlines.** Relativity predicts that clocks may tick differently relative to one another because of velocity or gravitational potential. This is experimentally established.

**P2: A physically realizable path can return an observer to an earlier spacetime event.** Mathematical spacetimes can permit closed timelike curves, but whether nature allows their formation and physical traversal is not established.

**P3: The world possesses multiple simultaneously realized quantum branches or distinct histories.** Some quantum interpretations describe branching reality; that description should not be conflated with an observable mechanism for travel between timelines. A physical experiment discriminating such models would need different predicted observations under defined conditions.

P1 neither implies P2 nor P3. P2, even if established in a hypothetical model, would not automatically establish P3.

## Source comparison
### Measured rates: Bothwell et al. (2022)
The atomic-clock experiment described by Bothwell and colleagues resolves gravitational redshift at millimetre scale (library SRC-005; related ledger WL-001). The accepted conclusion is a test of relativistic time dilation in a controlled apparatus, not a journey backwards in time. The results cannot on their own tell us the number of realized quantum histories.

Original: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-04349-7

### Mathematical possibilities: Gödel (1949) and Hawking (1992)
Gödel presents a cosmological solution to Einstein's equations associated with unusual global causal structure (SRC-002). Hawking studies constraints on the formation of chronology horizons and proposes chronology protection (SRC-003, WL-002/WL-003). The first concerns what particular equations permit under their conditions; the second argues there may be physical obstacles. Neither is observational evidence of an engineered time machine.

Originals: https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.21.447 and https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevD.46.603

### Self-consistency models: Deutsch (1991)
Deutsch investigates quantum systems near hypothetical closed timelike lines (SRC-019, WL-004). Such a model can illuminate fixed points and consistency rules. Its premises are not measurements of a macroscopic physical CTC. The abstract describes proposed nonstandard consequences, but the laboratory existence of the stipulated spacetime remains an open issue.

Original: https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevD.44.3197

### What counts as a quantum world? Everett (1957), Bell (1964), Zurek (2003)
Everett's relative-state paper (SRC-007) is the historical formal starting point for many-worlds interpretations. Bell's theorem (SRC-008) places constraints on local hidden-variable descriptions; a successful Bell test does **not** automatically favor Everett over all one-outcome nonlocal or collapse accounts. Zurek (SRC-011) explains how decoherence stabilizes classical-looking records but does not alone count worlds or decisively specify a unique ontology. The scholarly Stanford reference on many worlds (SRC-012) makes clear that interpreting the universal quantum formalism requires a further account of experienced worlds.

Originals: https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.29.454 | https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysicsPhysiqueFizika.1.195 | https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.75.715 | https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/

### Causal order is not the same as a history being rewritten
Oreshkov, Costa and Brukner (2012; SRC-020) study quantum correlations in a framework with no prior global causal order. This is conceptually significant, but the abstract alone does not show a person traversing an alternative past or rewriting a completed macroscopic history.

Original: https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms2076

## Early conclusion
The catalogue clarifies theory families and testable boundaries, but none of the examined summary-level sources demonstrates empirical access to multiple realized timelines. The hypothesis comparison is therefore unresolved. The inherited Truth Compass score remains unchanged at 59/100, expressly as an illustrative editorial orientation, not a measured chance of many worlds.

## What can falsify or distinguish?
A future comparison must specify *which* single-history or collapse/hidden-variable account, *which* Everettian or branching account, their shared initial conditions, and predictions in measurably separable observables. It must check whether the experiment really discriminates ontology rather than only confirming standard quantum predictions. Where experimentally equivalent formulations predict identical observable outcomes, an experiment at that level cannot settle the distinction.
